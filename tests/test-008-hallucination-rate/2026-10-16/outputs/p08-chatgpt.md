Article 99a does not exist in the current EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Therefore, it does not amend or override Article 22.

However, the European Commission's Digital Omnibus proposal of November 2025 includes changes to Article 22 that may be what you're referring to. These proposed changes would make it easier for companies to use automated decision-making in certain circumstances, while retaining important protections for individuals.&#x20;

## 1. What Article 22 currently requires

Article 22 GDPR protects individuals against decisions based solely on automated processing, including profiling, where those decisions produce legal effects or similarly significant consequences.

Examples include automated loan refusals, hiring decisions and decisions affecting access to important services.

Under the existing rules, such decisions are generally prohibited unless one of three exceptions applies:

1. Contractual necessity: The decision is necessary to enter into or perform a contract.
2. Legal authorisation: EU or Member State law permits the decision and provides appropriate safeguards.
3. Explicit consent: The individual explicitly agrees to the automated decision-making.

For decisions based on contractual necessity or explicit consent, companies must also provide safeguards, including opportunities for human intervention, expressing a point of view and contesting the decision.&#x20;

## 2. What the proposed changes would do

The Commission's proposal would clarify that an automated decision may satisfy the contractual necessity exception even when the same decision could also be made by a human.

This matters because a restrictive interpretation of contractual necessity can make it difficult for companies to justify fully automated processes where manual alternatives exist.

The proposal would also rephrase Article 22(1), moving from language framed as an individual's right not to be subject to certain automated decisions to language specifying when those decisions are permitted.

However, the proposal does not eliminate the three legal conditions or the obligation to protect individuals' rights.&#x20;

## 3. Main obligations for companies

Regardless of whether the proposed amendment is eventually adopted, companies must comply with the existing GDPR obligations.

| Obligation                      | What companies must do                                                                                                             |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Establish a legal justification | Identify an applicable Article 22 exception for significant solely automated decisions, as well as a lawful basis under Article 6. |
| Provide transparency            | Inform individuals about qualifying automated decision-making, its underlying logic, significance and expected consequences.       |
| Ensure human intervention       | Where Article 22(3) applies, provide a genuine opportunity for a person to review the decision.                                    |
| Enable challenges               | Allow affected individuals to express their views and contest automated decisions.                                                 |
| Protect sensitive data          | Observe Article 22(4)'s additional restrictions on decisions involving special categories of personal data.                        |
| Manage risks                    | Apply data protection principles, appropriate security measures and data protection impact assessments where required.             |
| Demonstrate compliance          | Maintain sufficient records and procedures to show that the processing and safeguards meet GDPR requirements.                      |

These obligations arise from Article 22 and related GDPR provisions, including Articles 5, 6, 13–15, 24, 25 and 35.&#x20;

## 4. Practical example

Consider a bank using AI to approve or reject loan applications without human involvement.

Under the existing Article 22, the bank must establish that this decision falls within one of the permitted exceptions. If relying on contractual necessity, it must justify why the decision is necessary for the contract.

Under the Commission's proposed amendment, the existence of a viable manual decision-making process would not, by itself, prevent the bank from relying on contractual necessity.

Nevertheless, the bank would still need to comply with the GDPR's other requirements, including transparency, safeguards against unfair processing and the applicable rights to human intervention and contestation.

## 5. Legal status as of 16 October 2026

The GDPR amendments in the Digital Omnibus remain under negotiation, not in force. On 11 October 2026, a planned Council discussion and vote on the compromise text did not take place.&#x20;

Bottom line: There is no operative GDPR Article 99a changing automated decision-making rules. The relevant proposed amendment is to Article 22 itself. For companies, its principal potential benefit is greater flexibility in using automated decisions for contractual purposes, not removal of transparency, human review or other data protection obligations.
