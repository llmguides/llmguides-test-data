I'll check the primary studies so the figures come from the papers themselves rather than from memory.

**Yes, fasting raises growth hormone (GH), but the evidence comes mostly from small studies of 1–5 day water-only fasts, not from typical intermittent-fasting schedules like 16:8.** Below are the main peer-reviewed human studies with the numbers you asked for. Where a paper didn't report a percentage, I calculated it from the values it published, and I've marked those.

**1. Ho et al., 1988, *Journal of Clinical Investigation***
- Participants: six normal adult men, studied on a control fed day and on the first and fifth days of a 5-day fast.
- Result: 24-hour integrated GH concentration rose from 2.82 to 8.75 µg·min/ml (P = 0.0002), and maximal pulse amplitude rose from 5.9 to 12.3 ng/ml.
- Percentage increase (my calculation): about **+210%** in 24-hour GH (roughly 3-fold) and about +108% in peak pulse height.
- Context: over the same fast, somatomedin C (IGF-1) fell from 1.31 to 0.77 U/ml.

**2. Hartman et al., 1992, *Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism***
- Participants: nine normal men, with blood sampled every 5 minutes over 24 hours on a fed day and on the second day of a fast.
- Result: two days of fasting produced a 5-fold increase in 24-hour GH production, from 78 to 371 µg per liter of distribution volume (P = 0.0001).
- Percentage increase (my calculation): about **+376%**.
- Context: IGF-1 concentrations were unchanged after 56 hours of fasting.

**3. Horne et al., 2025, *Frontiers in Endocrinology***
- Participants: 30 apparently healthy people (20 women, 10 men), randomized in a crossover design to a 24-hour water-only fast or a day of normal eating.
- Result: the percent increase depended on starting levels. People with lower baseline GH had a median increase of 1,225%, compared with 50.3% for those with higher baseline GH (p<0.001).
- Percentage increase: **+1,225% (low-baseline group) vs. +50.3% (high-baseline group)**, as medians.
- Context: the authors concluded fasting raised GH and that the change was independent of weight loss. This is from the Intermountain group, whose earlier conference results are the likely source of the widely repeated "1,300% in women, 2,000% in men" figures. Those earlier numbers were a meeting presentation, not this peer-reviewed paper.

**4. Moro et al., 2016, *Journal of Translational Medicine* (16:8 time-restricted eating)**
- Participants: 34 resistance-trained men, randomly assigned to time-restricted feeding or a normal diet for 8 weeks.
- Percentage increase in GH: **not reported**. GH itself wasn't among the measured hormones.
- Result: testosterone and IGF-1 fell significantly in the time-restricted group, with no changes in the normal-diet group.

**Caveats**

- **The studies are tiny.** They include 6, 9, and 30 participants, mostly men.
- **The fasts are longer than typical intermittent fasting.** They last 24 hours to 5 days. I didn't find a peer-reviewed study showing that daily 16:8 eating raises GH.
- **"Percent increase" is inflated by low starting values.** Fed-state GH is often near zero, so a small absolute rise becomes a huge percentage. The Horne paper shows this directly with its 1,225% vs. 50% split.
- **More GH during fasting doesn't mean more growth.** GH's growth effects largely work through IGF-1, and IGF-1 stayed the same or dropped in these studies. The researchers interpret the GH rise mainly as a way to spare protein and mobilize fat while food is absent.
