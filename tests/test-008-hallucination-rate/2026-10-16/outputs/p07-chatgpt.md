## 1. The central argument

Human judgment suffers from two fundamentally different kinds of error: bias and noise. We have paid enormous attention to bias while systematically underestimating the damage caused by noise.

- Bias is a systematic tendency to make errors in a particular direction. For example, a judge who consistently imposes unusually harsh sentences.
- Noise is unwanted variability in judgments that should be similar. For example, two judges giving radically different sentences to comparable defendants, without a relevant justification.

The distinction can be illustrated with a target:

Bias

Judgments cluster together but consistently miss the target.

Noise

Judgments scatter widely, even when the average is near the target.

The authors' deeper claim is that noise is not simply a problem of incompetent decision-makers. Even highly experienced professionals produce surprisingly inconsistent judgments.

Their proposed solution is decision hygiene: designing procedures that make judgments more consistent before individuals reach their final conclusions.

## 3. The most important studies behind the book

The argument draws on experimental psychology, behavioral economics, legal research, forecasting studies, and organizational decision-making research.

Study 1: The insurance underwriting experiment

Discussed in Chapters 2 and 7

An insurance company asked professional underwriters to independently assess the same hypothetical cases. The median difference between pairs of estimates was approximately 55%, despite expectations that the variation would be around 10%.

Significance: Expertise does not guarantee consistency. Organizations can measure this problem through a noise audit.

Study 2: Federal judicial sentencing

Chapters 1 and 6

A 1974 federal judicial study asked judges to evaluate the same hypothetical criminal cases and found substantial differences in whether imprisonment was appropriate and how long sentences should be.

Significance: Legal outcomes can depend on which judge hears the case, independently of legally relevant differences.

Study 3: Refugee Roulette and asylum decisions

Chapters 1 and 7

Research on U.S. immigration adjudication found enormous differences in asylum approval rates among judges. The book cites a comparison in which one judge granted approximately 5% of applications and another approximately 88%.

Subsequent research has also demonstrated influences from judges' prior decisions and judicial predispositions.

Significance: Differences in decision-makers can create severe problems of procedural fairness.

Study 4: Clinical judgment versus statistical models

Chapters 9–11

Drawing on the research tradition associated with psychologist Paul Meehl and later studies of actuarial prediction, the authors explain why simple, consistently applied statistical rules frequently match or outperform unaided expert predictions.

Significance: A model does not need to understand every subtlety of a case to be useful. Its consistency can itself be an advantage.

This does not establish that algorithms are universally superior or free of bias.

Study 5: Tetlock's forecasting research

Chapter 21

Philip Tetlock's forecasting research, including the Good Judgment Project, examines how people predict geopolitical outcomes. It finds meaningful differences in forecasting skill and supports techniques such as combining independent forecasts, updating predictions, and tracking accuracy.

Significance: Aggregation and calibrated probabilities can improve forecasts beyond unstructured expert intuition.&#x20;

Study 6: Contextual bias in forensic science

Chapter 20

Research on fingerprint identification and other forensic disciplines demonstrates that irrelevant case information can affect expert interpretations.

The resulting recommendation is to control the sequence in which analysts receive evidence, so their early assessments are not contaminated by later contextual information.

Significance: More information is not always better when it is introduced at the wrong stage.

A methodological qualification matters here: these are not all randomized experiments, and they do not establish identical causes of variability. Some are field observations, others are controlled judgment exercises, and still others assess predictive performance. The evidence that undesirable inconsistency exists is broader than the evidence that any single remedy will eliminate it.

## 4. What the authors recommend

The authors favor changing the decision-making process rather than simply instructing people to think more carefully.

Their main practical principles can be combined into a simple procedure:

Decision hygiene workflow

1\. Define the judgment criteria

Agree on what matters and how it will be scored before evaluating cases.

2\. Collect independent assessments

Avoid anchoring reviewers on the first opinion expressed.

3\. Evaluate components separately

Score evidence on defined dimensions instead of relying immediately on an overall impression.

4\. Aggregate and compare

Combine judgments systematically and investigate large disagreements.

5\. Make the final holistic judgment

Allow informed deliberation after the structured evidence has been considered.

Chapter 25 applies a more formal version of this approach, the Mediating Assessments Protocol, to complex strategic decisions.

## 5. An important limitation of the argument

One criticism worth taking seriously is that disagreement does not automatically imply error.

In judgments involving fairness, dignity, punishment, or social values, reasonable people can assign different weights to legitimate considerations. Reducing variability may produce consistency without producing better substantive outcomes.

The book acknowledges this tension in Part VI. There are also practical costs to imposing standardized procedures, and rigid rules can eliminate discretion that is genuinely valuable.

Consequently, the strongest version of the argument is not that all human variation should disappear. It is that organizations should measure unexplained variation, identify its sources, and reduce the portion that serves no legitimate purpose.

## Bottom line

Noise extends the intellectual project Kahneman began in Thinking, Fast and Slow.

Where that earlier book emphasizes why individual minds make predictable mistakes, Noise emphasizes why systems of otherwise capable people can produce wildly inconsistent decisions.

The most consequential insight is that better outcomes often require better-designed decision procedures, not just smarter or more experienced decision-makers.

For deeper reading, I'd prioritize Chapters 2, 6–8, 9–11, 19, and 24–25. Together they cover how to detect noise, why it arises, what predictive models can contribute, and how to make professional judgments more consistent.
