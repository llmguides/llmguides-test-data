**Discoverers:** Tennessine was discovered by a Russian–American collaboration: the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, Vanderbilt University and Oak Ridge National Laboratory (2010). The experiments were carried out at JINR in Dubna, Russia. Discovery experiments were conducted using the Dubna Gas-Filled Recoil Separator together with the heavy-ion accelerator of the Flerov Laboratory of Nuclear Reactions. The team first reported its discovery in April 2010. The University of Tennessee, Knoxville, took part in later experiments that confirmed the discovery.

**Official confirmation: December 30, 2015.** On that date, IUPAC and the International Union of Pure and Applied Physics (IUPAP) announced that they had verified the existence of superheavy element 117. The verification was made by their Joint Working Party, which recognized the element and gave priority to the Russian–American team.

**Name approval: November 28, 2016.** The discoverers proposed the name, and IUPAC released it for public comment on June 8, 2016. On 28 November 2016, IUPAC approved the names and symbols for elements 113, 115, 117 and 118, including tennessine (Ts). This followed five months of public review, and the final approval came from the IUPAC Bureau. IUPAC published the announcement on November 30, 2016. The name honors the Tennessee region's contribution to superheavy element research, including Oak Ridge National Laboratory, Vanderbilt University, and the University of Tennessee at Knoxville.

**Target and supplier:** The target was **berkelium-249**, bombarded with a calcium-48 beam. It was supplied by **Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL)**. ORNL's main role in the discovery was producing the berkelium-249 for the search. It was made in the research reactor at DOE's Oak Ridge National Laboratory, the High Flux Isotope Reactor.
