The interesting twist is that Newfoundland actually switched from driving on the left to driving on the right on January 2, 1947 — more than two years before it joined Canada on March 31, 1949.

It wasn't a condition imposed by Canada as part of Confederation. Newfoundland made the decision independently, largely for practical reasons connected with North American traffic, American military bases, and the increasing number of North American vehicles on its roads.&#x20;

## Why Newfoundland changed sides

Newfoundland had traditionally followed British road rules. As a separate British dominion, it drove on the left, just like Britain.

But by the 1940s, there were compelling reasons to change.

- American military bases: During the Second World War, American forces established major bases at St. John's, Argentia, and Stephenville. Traffic inside those bases drove on the right, while traffic outside generally drove on the left. Drivers had to switch rules when entering or leaving.
- North American vehicles: American and Canadian vehicles were becoming more common in Newfoundland. Many had steering wheels on the left, making them better suited to right-side traffic.
- Regional consistency: Mainland Canadian provinces had already adopted right-side driving. Newfoundland was increasingly out of step with the rest of North America.
- Changing economic and cultural connections: Newfoundland's relationship with North America was becoming more important, particularly after the Second World War.

There had even been some right-side driving on parts of the Avalon Peninsula during the war, so the practice wasn't entirely unfamiliar.&#x20;

Late 1946 — Public information campaign

Newspapers carried repeated notices explaining the rules. There were discussions of car stickers, road markings, and special practice areas. Officials changed the starting date to January 2, partly over concerns about drivers being affected by New Year's celebrations.

January 2, 1947 — The changeover

The amended traffic law took effect just after midnight. All road users were expected to follow the new rule immediately. Police were deployed to busy junctions, including Rawlins Cross in St. John's.

## What made it difficult in practice?

The biggest challenges weren't necessarily the cars themselves.

1\. Buses suddenly had doors on the wrong side. Many buses had their passenger doors on the left, which had previously faced the pavement. After the change, those doors faced moving traffic. Golden Arrow Coaches warned customers that they would have to board and disembark in the street. Signs were placed on the backs of buses to warn following drivers.

2\. Horses had learned the old rules. Newfoundland still had considerable horse-drawn traffic in 1947. Some horses instinctively headed toward the left side of the road, even when their drivers directed them right.

3\. Pedestrians had to relearn road safety. People walking along roads without pavements were encouraged to walk on the left so they could face approaching traffic.

4\. People worried about winter conditions. January meant snow and ice, with road markings potentially obscured. There were calls to postpone the change until spring, but the government proceeded.

5\. Public transport was temporarily disrupted. Bus and taxi services finished early on January 1. According to a contemporary newspaper account, taxis became so scarce that a person needing hospital transport had to be taken in a police van.&#x20;

## Did the change cause chaos?

Surprisingly, no.

The Evening Telegram reported that motorists initially drove more slowly than usual, but gradually regained confidence as the day progressed.

One policeman reported that a few drivers were still drifting toward the left, but they were a small minority. Horses appeared somewhat confused, yet the feared widespread collisions and disorder did not materialize.

There was no need to replace every car or relocate every steering wheel. Newfoundland already had a mixture of left- and right-hand-steering vehicles. The immediate task was to change where vehicles travelled, not where their drivers sat.

The local press described the transition as an “unobtrusive debut.”&#x20;

## Was there a connection to joining Canada?

Yes, but it was more symbolic and practical than legal.

In fact, an Evening Telegram editorial published on the day of the change suggested that driving on the right might symbolize the new constitutional direction Newfoundland was considering.&#x20;
