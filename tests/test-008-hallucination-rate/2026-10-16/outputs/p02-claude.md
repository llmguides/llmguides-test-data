A small correction first: the switch didn't come with Confederation. Newfoundland, which didn't join Confederation until 1949, continued to drive on the left side of the road until 1947. It changed on January 2, 1947, about two years before becoming a province, while it was still under Britain's appointed Commission of Government.

**Why it switched**

Newfoundland was the last holdout in the region. Canada started a move toward universal right-side driving in the 1920s, and Newfoundland and Labrador were the laggards. Being an island with close ties to Britain had kept it on the left.

The Second World War pushed things along. American military bases were treated as American soil, so the bases in Stephenville, Argentia and St. John's already drove on the right, and they brought in a lot of vehicles built for right-hand traffic. Canadian servicemen were also used to the right. In one 1945 case near Torbay, an ex-soldier with only an army licence caused a head-on collision by driving on the right. By then the island's car fleet was a mix of left- and right-hand-drive vehicles. Most people seemed to accept that, given how close it was to mainland North America, Newfoundland would be better off driving on the right.

There was a symbolic side as well. The Evening Telegram suggested at the time that the change might reflect the new constitutional order Newfoundland was considering. In hindsight, it was a small step away from Britain and toward North America that came just before the much larger one.

**How the changeover worked**

- **Long notice:** In February 1946 the government announced that Newfoundland would officially adopt 'drive to the right' on January 1st, 1947. That gave people almost 11 months to get used to the idea.
- **Arguments about the date:** Some people thought switching in winter, on icy roads, was a mistake and proposed April instead. The government didn't budge on the season. It did accept a complaint that New Year's Day, the morning after the night before, was a bad choice, so it moved the date to January 2.
- **Publicity:** In the week before the change, "Drive Slowly, Drive Safely, Drive Right" ads appeared on almost every page of the newspapers. There was also talk of handing out car stickers.
- **Driver worries:** People driving right-hand-drive cars had learned to judge their position by watching the ditch. They feared drifting off the road or into oncoming traffic, and someone suggested painting a centre line on the highways.
- **Everyone else on the road:** Pedestrians were told to walk on the left so they would face oncoming traffic. Buses were an awkward case: a St. John's bus company warned that its doors would now open on the traffic side, so passengers would have to get on and off in the street. People also worried that horses trained to keep left would wander into traffic. Others pointed out that many horses had come from the mainland and had already switched once.
- **The day itself:** The new rule took effect at 12:01 a.m. on January 2, 1947. St. John's sent extra police to Rawlins Cross, one of its busiest intersections. According to the Telegram, traffic moved slowly that morning and sped up as the novelty wore off. Only a few cars stayed on the left, and a few old city horses looked confused. The paper called it an unremarkable debut.

So when Newfoundland joined Canada in 1949, its roads already matched the rest of the country's.
