The likely mix-up is with ***Noise: A Flaw in Human Judgment*** (2021), which Kahneman co-wrote with Olivier Sibony and Cass Sunstein.

**Main argument of *Noise***

Kahneman's earlier work focused on *bias*, meaning judgments that are wrong in a consistent direction. *Noise* argues there's a second, neglected source of error: *noise*, which is unwanted variability in judgments that should be identical. Two judges sentencing the same defendant, two underwriters pricing the same risk, or one doctor reading the same scan on different days can reach very different conclusions. The authors argue that noise is often as large as bias or larger, that organizations rarely measure it, and that it can be reduced.

**Key ideas, roughly following the book's structure**

- **Bias vs. noise.** Error is split into a systematic part (bias) and a scatter part (noise), illustrated with a target-shooting analogy. In a mean-squared-error framework, both contribute equally to total error.
- **Types of noise.** *Level noise* is when some judges are consistently harsher than others. *Pattern noise* is when judges react idiosyncratically to particular cases. *Occasion noise* is when the same person's judgment shifts with mood, time of day, or weather.
- **Noise audits.** Organizations can measure noise by giving several professionals the same cases. In an insurance company the authors studied, executives guessed premiums would vary by about 10% between underwriters. The actual median difference was around 55%.
- **Prediction and its limits.** Simple rules and statistical models often beat expert judgment, largely because they're noise-free. Even a model of a judge's own decisions can outperform that judge. The authors also stress "objective ignorance": many outcomes simply can't be predicted well.
- **Decision hygiene.** Their remedies include using independent multiple judges and aggregating their views, structuring decisions into separate assessments, delaying intuition until the evidence is in, using relative scales rather than absolute ones, and their "Mediating Assessments Protocol."

**Studies and evidence it draws on**

- Research on criminal sentencing disparities, including Judge Marvin Frankel's critique and studies showing judges' sentences varying with factors like weather or local football results.
- Asylum decision studies showing grant rates depending heavily on which judge hears the case.
- The authors' own noise audits in insurance underwriting and claims adjusting.
- Clinical-vs-statistical prediction research going back to Paul Meehl.
- Medical diagnosis variability, such as radiologists disagreeing with one another and with themselves.
- "Wisdom of crowds" and forecasting research, including Philip Tetlock's superforecasters.
- The Fragile Families Challenge, which showed how poorly life outcomes can be predicted even with rich data.
