Newfoundland actually switched from driving on the left to driving on the right at midnight on **January 2, 1947**—more than two years *before* it joined Canada on March 31, 1949. At the time, Newfoundland was still governed by the British-appointed Commission of Government.

While the change was not a direct requirement of Confederation, it was driven by the same forces pulling the island away from the British orbit and toward mainland North America.

---

## Why Newfoundland Made the Switch

* **North American Vehicle Imports:** Although Newfoundland followed British traffic laws, geography dictated that almost all of its cars, trucks, and buses were imported from the United States and Canada. Because these vehicles were built for right-hand traffic (with the steering wheel on the left), driving them on the left side of the road put the driver on the curbside rather than the centerline, severely limiting visibility when passing. Worse, buses imported from the mainland had passenger doors on the right, forcing riders to board and exit directly into the middle of the street.
* **The World War II Military Boom:** During WWII, Newfoundland became a strategic Allied hub, hosting tens of thousands of American and Canadian troops at bases in St. John's, Argentia, Stephenville, and Gander. Under the 1941 Leased Bases Agreement, U.S. military bases operated under American law—meaning traffic drove on the right inside the gates and switched to the left as soon as it hit local roads. The massive influx of North American military vehicles made left-hand driving increasingly impractical and accustomed locals to right-hand rules.
* **Catching Up with the Maritimes:** Canada's Maritime provinces (New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, and Prince Edward Island) and British Columbia had already switched from left to right between 1922 and 1924 to standardize trade and tourism with the United States and central Canada. By 1946, Newfoundland was the lone holdout in North America.

## How the Changeover Worked in Practice

When the government amended the *Highway Traffic Act* in February 1946, it gave the public nearly 11 months to prepare. Executing the switch required solving several quirky logistical problems:

### 1. The "New Year's Hangover" Postponement

The government originally scheduled the changeover for **January 1, 1947**. However, as the date approached, citizens and newspaper editors pointed out a glaring flaw: asking drivers to unlearn a lifetime of muscle memory on New Year's Day—when many motorists (and horse drivers) would be intoxicated or nursing severe hangovers from New Year's Eve—was a recipe for disaster. The government relented and pushed the effective time back 24 hours to **12:01 a.m. on January 2, 1947**.

### 2. Using Winter to Limit Traffic

Many residents wrote to newspapers arguing that switching in April would be safer than switching on icy, snow-covered January roads. In practice, winter worked to the authorities' advantage. Outside the capital of St. John's and a few larger towns, rural roads were snowed in and closed for the season. Even in town, many private car owners put their vehicles up on blocks for the winter, meaning the transition happened when motor traffic was at its absolute lowest volume of the year.

### 3. The Media Blitz and Transit Adjustments

Throughout December 1946, newspapers like *The Evening Telegram* and *The Daily News* printed urgent reminder ads on almost every page with the slogan: **"Drive Slowly, Drive Safely, Drive Right."**

* **Pedestrians** were targeted with a parallel campaign—*"Walk Left"*—teaching them to look the opposite way before stepping off curbs and to walk facing oncoming traffic on country roads.
* **Buses** were outfitted with large warning signs on their rear bumpers reminding drivers not to instinctively swerve to the right to overtake them.

### 4. Retraining the Horses

In 1947, horse-drawn sleighs, carts, and delivery wagons still shared the streets with automobiles. Teamsters worried that horses—habituated to keeping to the left automatically—would panic or drift into oncoming traffic. Fortunately, a large proportion of Newfoundland's working horses had originally been purchased from farms in mainland Canada and the United States. Because those horses had been broken in to walk on the right before arriving on the island, they readapted with minimal fuss.

### 5. Rollout Day: January 2, 1947

On the morning of the switch, extra traffic police were stationed at major St. John's intersections like Rawlins Cross. Many nervous drivers—including a large share of the city's taxi fleet—simply stayed home to watch how the first day played out, causing a brief cab shortage that required police vans to transport patients to the hospital.

Aside from a few minor fender-benders caused by instinctive turns at intersections, the predicted chaos never materialized. *The Evening Telegram* called it an "unobtrusive debut," noting that St. John's drivers took to the right side of the road "like the royal swans took to the boatpool in Bowring Park."
